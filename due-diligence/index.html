Due Diligence in North Carolina: Fees, Earnest Money, and Deadlines

North Carolina purchases work differently from most states. When your offer is accepted, you usually pay two separate amounts, and each one behaves differently if you walk away. This page explains both and how I structure them in an offer.

The Window

How the due diligence period works

The standard North Carolina contract is the Offer to Purchase and Contract, Form 2-T. When it takes effect, a negotiated due diligence period begins. During that window you inspect the home, order the appraisal, finalize your loan, review the survey and HOA documents, and check anything else that matters to your decision.

Until the deadline, you can terminate for any reason or no reason, and your earnest money comes back to you. Termination must be in writing and delivered before the due diligence deadline, which comes before the closing date. After the deadline passes, you are expected to close.

Schedule inspections at the start of the period. Findings, repair quotes, and loan answers all need to arrive before the deadline so you can renegotiate, proceed, or terminate while your earnest money is still protected.

How I Structure Offers

How much due diligence to offer

No law sets a minimum or maximum fee. The right amount depends on how competitive the listing is, the price, the home’s age and condition, how long it has been on the market, and how much cash you are willing to put at risk.

When there is little competition, I try to get you under contract with as little due diligence money as possible. A small fee means less money lost if the inspection turns up a problem, and it gives you more leverage in repair negotiations because you can walk away without losing much.

In a multiple-offer situation, I may advise a larger fee than you planned. Sellers keep the fee even if you terminate, so a larger fee tells them you are committed, and it often decides between offers at similar prices.

Rural properties around Fuquay-Varina, Willow Spring, and parts of Johnston County often need a longer due diligence period for well, septic, and survey work that an in-town home does not need. Ask for that time when you make the offer.

Avoid These

Common due diligence mistakes

Mixing up the two amounts

The fee goes to the seller and the earnest money goes to escrow. They are returned under different rules.

Treating the deadline as flexible

Missing the due diligence deadline can put your earnest money at risk.

Scheduling inspections late

Waiting until the end of the period leaves no time to get repair quotes or negotiate.

Ending it by phone

A verbal “we’re out” is not a termination. It must be in writing and delivered on time.

Copying a percentage from the internet

Fees are set listing by listing. A flat formula can overpay on a quiet listing or lose a contested one.

Waiving inspections to win

Skipping inspections to compete can leave you with expensive problems and no protection.
