What land in Fuquay-Varina has sold for

Wake County recorded 97 qualified land sales inside the Fuquay-Varina planning jurisdiction between November 1, 2024 and October 1, 2026, covering 94 different parcels. A qualified sale is one a county appraiser reviewed as an arm's-length transaction, so foreclosures and sales between relatives are generally left out. Of the 97 sales, 68 were inside the town limits and 29 were outside the town limits but inside the area where the town handles zoning and planning.

The median sale price was $182,000, and the median parcel was 0.77 acres. Sales ranged from $5,000 to $8,450,000, and parcels ranged from 0.08 to 39.35 acres. In the current county record, 25 of the 97 sales were of parcels that now have a house on them, so some of these sales were lots bought to build on.

Before you buy

What to check before you buy land in Fuquay-Varina

Survey. Order a boundary survey from a licensed North Carolina surveyor. It confirms the acreage, the corners, road frontage, easements, and anything built across a property line. The acreage in the tax record is not a survey.

Perc test and septic. If the parcel will not connect to town sewer, the soil has to pass an evaluation for a septic system before you can build. Wake County issues septic permits, and the evaluation can limit where the house goes and how many bedrooms it can have. Start the Wake County septic permit application during your due diligence period, because it can take weeks.

Water. Ask whether town water and sewer reach the property and what it would cost to connect. If not, you will need a private well. Wake County permits new wells, and its wells and septic systems page lists the steps and fees.

Zoning and planning. Confirm the zoning, the minimum lot size, setbacks, and whether you can divide the land later. Inside the town limits and the town's planning area, contact the Fuquay-Varina Planning Department. Outside it, contact Wake County Planning and Development Services. The town's maps and GIS page shows town limits and zoning.

Flood map. Check the FEMA flood map for the parcel. Land in a flood zone can be harder to build on and may require flood insurance. Stream buffers and wetlands can also reduce the area you can use.

Covenants and HOA rules. A lot in a subdivision may have recorded covenants on home size, building materials, fences, and outbuildings, and an HOA with dues. Ask for them before you make an offer.

My North Carolina due diligence guide covers how the due diligence fee, earnest money, and deadlines work. Plan your due diligence period around how long the survey, soil evaluation, and planning questions will take.
